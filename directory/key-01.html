The Key

Your numbers have relationships.

Everyone carries four frequencies. Set one beside someone else’s and the two make an interval, the same intervals music has been naming for centuries. This page is the key to reading them.

How it works

Two tones, one distance.

A pitch is just a number: cycles per second, written Hz. Put two tones together and what the ear actually hears is neither number. It hears the distance between them.

Music measures that distance in cents. One octave is 1,200 cents wide, and the named intervals sit at fixed places along it. When two tones land within 25 cents of one of those places, the pair carries that interval’s name.

The field guide

Twelve names, and one that is not an interval at all.

A reading is built in three registers, always in this order: first whether the two tones are the same note, then the strongest harmony between them, then one honest tension. Here is everything that can appear.

Outside every register

Golden

Not a musical interval at all. It is nature’s own proportion, the ratio that never resolves into a whole-number relationship, the one the sunflower and the nautilus are built on.

The connection that keeps unfolding. It does not settle, and that is the gift. Golden is recognised inside a window eight times tighter than any named interval, which is what makes it the rarest thing on this page.

A mark, not an interval

Across an octave. The two tones sit more than an octave apart, so the relationship holds but is expressed in different registers. 16 of 100 pairs carry it somewhere.

Putting it together

How to read a pairing.

Sixteen pathways produce a lot of information. A reading keeps only three things, in a fixed order, so that two people always get the same sentence from the same numbers.

01

The sameness stamp

Do any of the sixteen land on the same note? Unison and octave both count, and they are not ranked against each other. Sung together they make the chorus effect: one voice, thickened. About half of all pairs carry a stamp somewhere.

02

The harmony headline

The strongest harmony present, taken straight down the ladder: fifth, fourth, major third, major sixth, minor third, minor sixth. If several pathways carry it, the tightest one leads.

03

One named tension

Exactly one, and it is described as a direction rather than a problem. Tension is what makes music move; nothing here is a warning.

What that sounds like
“Her Vitality makes a Perfect Fifth with your Love. Her aliveness warms how you love, and it is the most stable harmony in music.”

Pathway plus interval, in one sentence. The tension line follows it, never replaces it.

The one rule

A key, not a verdict.

Nothing here says two people are right or wrong for each other. A fifth is not a score, and a minor second is not a warning. Music has always used tension as direction. It is the reason a piece moves at all. Read these the same way.
